What do these numbers mean?

Lden — "day-evening-night" level: the average aircraft noise across a full 24 h, but with evening flights (19–23h) counted +5 dB louder and night flights (23–07h) +10 dB louder, because they disturb more. It's the EU's standard indicator of long-term annoyance. WHO guideline for aircraft: below 45 dB.

Lnight — the average noise during the night (23:00–07:00), used to judge sleep disturbance. WHO guideline: below 40 dB.

Peak — the loudest single modelled flyover at a spot (not an average): how loud it gets when a plane passes over.

Levels are A-weighted decibels (dBA). Decibels are logarithmic: +10 dB ≈ twice as loud, +3 dB is just noticeable. Rough guide: 40 quiet library · 55 light traffic · 70 vacuum cleaner · 85 city street.

Lden/Lnight are averages over the of flight data — a long-term picture, not one day.

These are modelled estimates from ADS-B flight tracks, not yet calibrated to the official noise sensors — read them as indicative and comparative, not exact.

How is the noise calculated?

There are no microphones here — noise is modelled from each aircraft's position, altitude and climb/descent, sampled every few seconds from its ADS-B signal. For every tracked moment we estimate how loud it is at a location from:

• Distance — level falls off with the straight-line distance to the plane (spreading + air absorption); one at 300 m is far louder than one at 3 km.

• Engine thrust — estimated from how hard the aircraft is working — its rate of gaining height and speed — so a jet powering off the runway is modelled far louder than one gliding down to land, and the noise curve is read off at that thrust.

• Aircraft type — where a measured noise-vs-distance reference curve is available for the model it is used; other types use a generic jet model.

• Off to the side — planes passing to one side are quieter than ones directly overhead (ground shielding), via the SAE-AIR-5662 lateral-attenuation model.

These per-moment levels are energy-averaged over the whole period into Lden / Lnight (with the evening/night penalties), and the loudest single pass gives Peak.

It's a physics estimate, not calibrated to the real noise monitors around the airport — best for comparing locations, not as exact dB readings.
